Apple Pay Deposits

This project is bringing Apple Pay to The National Lottery as a new way to deposit, with Google Pay planned to follow. As sole designer on the Payments team, I've designed and tested the flows in a product where each account can only have one linked payment method and anti-money laundering (AML) rules can decide which card a player may use.

My role: Research, Information architecture, user flow architecture, UX/UI design, prototyping, user testing

The Problem

Adding Apple Pay isn't just adding a button. A player can only have one payment method on their account, so paying with Apple Pay could mean replacing their saved debit card. For players with an active AML rule, the Apple Pay card also has to match the debit card already on their account. The design has to make both rules clear at the moment of payment, without turning a quick deposit into a slow one.

The Solution

I designed three journeys: a new player with no card saved, an existing player whose debit card is replaced by Apple Pay, and a player with an active AML rule. Each one explains its rule where it matters, through notes on the deposit screen, a confirmation once the card is removed, and a clear error state when an Apple Pay card doesn't match. I had weekly calls with Apple's team to work through challenges as they came up and get their advice on the designs.

Accessibility

The yellow and blue notices carry critical information, like the card-matching warning and the note that a saved debit card will be removed, so every variation had to meet WCAG AAA contrast. The navy text on the cream warning, for example, has a contrast ratio of 15.08:1. Each notice also uses an icon, so the message doesn't rely on colour alone.

Designing for the system, not just the flow

Apple Pay needed a way to explain a rule at the moment of payment, so I designed a new banner component. I worked alongside our design system designer and followed the agreed contribution process.

Foundations

I built it entirely from existing colour, type and spacing tokens, so it stays on-brand and updates automatically with the system.

Variants

A yellow warning for the AML message, “Cards need to match”, which players need to act on, and a blue note explaining what happens when a card is removed. Separating “you need to do something” from “here’s useful context” lets players tell at a glance how serious a message is. The illustration, the same on web and app, helps the banner stand out, tackling the banner blindness we saw with the toast, and makes a regulatory message feel friendlier.

Scalability

Although Apple Pay was the trigger, I designed it so it wasn’t tied to one flow. With small adaptations it’s now used on our draw-based game pages, and once it had proved itself there, we moved it into the design system’s components for global use. Because it was already built from existing tokens, that move was smooth.

The Research

We tested the prototypes over three rounds of unmoderated usability testing, with around 90 participants in total, and changed the design between rounds. The prototypes were built end to end, including card entry and the Apple Pay sheet itself, which is likely why some participants tried to trigger the real Apple Pay using their phone's side button.

  • 70–77% preferred paying with Apple Pay, in every round
  • 20/20 completed a first Apple Pay deposit (round 3)
  • 30% → 63% noticed the AML warning after we moved and restyled it (3 of 10 → 12 of 19)

New player

First deposits felt quick and easy, and the Apple Pay button stood out. No changes were needed.

Card replaced

Everyone completed the deposit, but most didn't notice their debit card had been removed. It mattered less than expected: 90% said it wouldn't affect them, and most assumed it was for security.

Active AML

Players understood the failed payment message but missed the warning before it, until we moved and restyled it. When a payment failed, about half fixed it by switching card, and three in four said they'd use their debit card if the matching card wasn't in their wallet.

What testing shaped

  • Moved and restyled the "Cards need to match" warning so more players notice it before they pay
  • Found the "debit card removed" splash screen didn't appear to improve the experience
  • Recommended linking the deposits and payments copy to a fraud and security FAQ

Where it stands

Apple Pay is in development and due to launch in mid-December, with Google Pay planned to follow. Google Pay will build on the same flows and AML rules, which should make it quicker to design and test.
